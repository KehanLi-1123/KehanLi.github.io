## Standard Market Convention

- At the **start date** ($$T_0$$):  
  - Each party exchanges a **notional principal** in their domestic currency.  
  - The **spot FX rate prevailing at $$T_0$$** is used to set the equivalent notionals across the two legs.  
    - Example:  
      - USD leg notional = USD 100m  
      - EUR leg notional = $$100\text{m} \times \text{spot(EUR/USD)}$$  

- During the life of the swap:  
  - Each party pays interest in its own currency (usually floating, e.g., 3M USD SOFR vs 3M EURIBOR + basis).  
  - There is **no further principal exchange** until maturity.  

- At **maturity** ($$T_N$$):  
  - The **same notionals as at inception** are re-exchanged (no FX risk at maturity is built into the swap).  
  - That is, the USD 100m is returned, and the EUR notional (set using the **spot FX at $$T_0$$**) is given back.  

### Key Points
- The **FX rate is locked at the start**. Both the initial and final exchanges are based on the spot FX at $$T_0$$.  
- This ensures the swap is **zero-value at inception** (ignoring the basis spread).  
- The mark-to-market (MtM) during the life of the swap reflects changes in:  
  - Interest rate differentials,  
  - Cross-currency basis spreads,  
  - The **spot FX forward curve** (since the final principal exchange is locked at $$T_0$$, not at the forward FX).  

---

## Who Is the USD Borrower?

- The **USD borrower** is the party who **receives USD principal at inception**.  
- That party then:  
  - Pays USD floating interest (e.g. SOFR + spread),  
  - Repays the USD notional at maturity.  
- Conversely, the USD lender delivers USD at inception, receives USD interest, and gets USD back at maturity.  

---

## Why Australia Is an Offshore Borrower of AUD

- Australia has historically run a **current account deficit (CAD)**, meaning it **imports more than it exports** and must **borrow from abroad**.  
- Foreign investors are willing to provide this funding because:  
  - AUD is a **commodity-linked currency**, seen as a proxy for global growth and China’s demand.  
  - AUD offers **higher yields** than G3 currencies (USD, EUR, JPY).  
  - Central banks and sovereign wealth funds include AUD in reserves.  

- As a result, offshore investors buy **AUD-denominated bonds** issued by Australian banks and corporates.  
- This makes Australian banks **reliant on offshore AUD funding**.  

But banks also need USD (for trade, global investments, USD assets).  
- They **swap offshore AUD funding into USD** via xccy swaps.  
- This creates **excess supply of USD** in the swap market.  

👉 Outcome: USD becomes **cheap vs AUD**, so the AUD leg must **pay a positive basis spread** → **AUD/USD basis > 0**.  

---

## Why Japan Is a Capital Exporter

- Japan has run a **persistent current account surplus (CAS)** for decades.  
- This reflects its export-driven economy, strong domestic savings, and low domestic investment returns.  
- Household savings and corporate profits are recycled into overseas investments through:  
  - Life insurers and pension funds (buying USD and EUR bonds),  
  - The GPIF (world’s largest pension fund),  
  - Banks and corporates investing abroad.  

- These institutions raise funds in JPY domestically, then **swap JPY into USD** to buy USD-denominated assets.  
- This creates **huge demand for USD funding** in the swap market.  

👉 Outcome: USD becomes **expensive vs JPY**, so the JPY leg must **pay a negative basis spread** → **JPY/USD basis < 0**.  

---

## Cheap vs. Expensive USD in Swaps

- **AUD/USD basis positive:**  
  - More demand for **AUD** funding.  
  - Offshore AUD supply swapped into USD → USD cheap vs AUD.  

- **JPY/USD basis negative:**  
  - More demand for **USD** funding.  
  - Japanese investors swap JPY into USD → USD expensive vs JPY.  

---

## Big Picture Intuition

- **Net capital exporters into USD (JPY, EUR):** basis is usually **negative** because of strong **USD demand**.  
- **Currencies attractive to offshore investors (AUD):** basis is often **positive** because of strong **AUD demand**.  

---

✅ **Summary:**  
- Notionals are exchanged at **spot FX** on day one and reversed at maturity using the **same spot FX**.  
- The **USD borrower** is the one who **receives USD principal at inception**.  
- **AUD basis positive → more demand for AUD → USD cheap.**  
- **JPY basis negative → more demand for USD → USD expensive.**  
- Structural flows explain it: Australia relies on **offshore AUD borrowing**, Japan is a **net capital exporter into USD assets**.
