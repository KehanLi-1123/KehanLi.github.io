Inflation and Pension Scheme Benefits
What parts of a pension are inflation-linked?
1) Before retirement (deferred revaluation)
⦁	When a member leaves service (or the scheme closes), their accrued pension is revalued each year up to retirement.
⦁	Typical rule: CPI-linked revaluation with a cap, often by accrual tranche (e.g., “pre-2009: CPI max 5%; 2009–2011: CPI max 2.5%,” etc.).
⦁	GMP and excess over GMP can have different statutory rules. GMP has its own revaluation basis (fixed rate or section 148 orders), and excess typically follows CPI with caps.
2) After retirement (pensions in payment increases)
⦁	Many schemes increase pensions each year in line with an index—CPI or RPI, often with a cap (and sometimes a floor).
⦁	Common patterns (by service tranche):
⦁	Pre-97: often scheme-specific (some discretionary, some fixed/RPI/CPI with caps).
⦁	1997–2005: “LPI 5%” (index-linked with a 5% cap).
⦁	Post-2005: “LPI 2.5%” (index-linked with a 2.5% cap).
⦁	Some schemes hard-code RPI; others allow switching to CPI (subject to rules/trust deed wording and case law).
What does “capped/floored” mean?
If the rule says “CPI capped at 5% (floor 0%),” the annual pension increase is:
[
\text{Increase} = \min\big(\max(\text{CPI},,0%),,5%\big)
]
So very high inflation won’t fully pass through (the cap bites), and negative CPI won’t reduce the pension if there’s a 0% floor.
Why inflation matters for valuation
⦁	Future cashflows depend on compound increases from these rules. Higher expected inflation ⇒ larger benefits ⇒ higher liabilities.
⦁	Caps and floors mean the effective expected increase is less than pure inflation when inflation is high (cap binds) and not less than zero if there’s a floor. Actuaries must model the option-like effect of caps/floors.
How actuaries set the inflation assumptions (practical approach)
⦁	Start from market-implied inflation (e.g., break-evens from index-linked vs nominal gilts, or inflation swaps).
⦁	Convert to the scheme’s index (RPI → CPI) using a wedge (long-term RPI–CPI differential).
⦁	Adjust for caps/floors: either
⦁	a deterministic haircut to expected increases, or
⦁	a market-consistent approach that explicitly values the cap/floor option (more common in buy-in/buy-out or accounting/MCF work, less so in funding unless material).
Nuances by benefit slice
⦁	A single member’s pension can be split into tranches, each with different increase rules (e.g., pre-97 fixed 3%, 97–05 LPI 5%, post-05 LPI 2.5%).
⦁	GMP in payment often has special rules: part may have fixed 3% increases (post-88 GMP up to 3% CPI paid by the scheme), while pre-88 GMP increases are typically paid by the State, not the scheme. The excess follows the scheme’s increase rule.
Short example
⦁	Accrued pension at retirement: £10,000.
⦁	Rule: CPI with cap 3%, floor 0% (LPI 3%).
⦁	If expected long-term CPI = 3.5%, you won’t assume 3.5% for increases—because the cap bites. The expected increase is about 3%, slightly less if you allow for inflation volatility (cap option value).
⦁	If expected CPI = 1%, expected increase is 1% (the cap doesn’t bind).
Investment/hedging angle (why sponsors care)
⦁	Because liabilities move with inflation expectations, schemes often use LDI and inflation hedges (linker gilts, inflation swaps) to reduce funding volatility. The more benefits that are inflation-linked (and the higher the caps), the stronger the inflation sensitivity.
