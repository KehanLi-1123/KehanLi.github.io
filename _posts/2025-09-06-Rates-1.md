## Yield Decomposition

A long-maturity nominal yield $$y_t(n)$$ can be decomposed as:

$$
y_t(n) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t_i}(r_{t+i}) + \mathrm{TP}_t(n).
$$

- **Expected future short rates**: market expectations of the average policy rate over the bond’s life.  
- **Term premium**: extra compensation for holding long-duration bonds instead of rolling short-term instruments.

The BIS note highlights that long yields declined **not** due to changes in expected future short rates, but because the **term premium compressed** (sometimes even negative).

---

## Why Did the Term Premium Compress?

1. **ECB QE expectations (asset purchases)**  
   Large, price-insensitive buying of long-duration bonds reduced the free float of duration.  
   By the **portfolio-balance channel**, less compensation was required for holding what remained.

2. **Easy funding conditions**  
   LTRO/TLTRO and negative deposit rates provided abundant liquidity.  
   Carry/roll-down trades were attractive, so dealers accepted lower term premia.

3. **Regulatory and liability-driven demand**  
   Insurers and pension funds needed long-duration assets for liability matching (e.g., Solvency II, LDI).  
   QE + limited issuance shrank supply while demand persisted → term premia fell.

4. **Reduced macro tail risks**  
   Lower break-up or banking-stress risks meant less risk compensation required.

---

## Why This Indicates “Unusually Strong Demand”

If expected policy rates are roughly stable but long yields fall, the drop must come from lower term premia.  
That means investors are willing to **accept less compensation for duration risk** → stronger demand for long bonds.

---

## Toy Example

Suppose the 10y yield falls from 2.00% to 1.50%.  
Surveys show expected short rates move from ≈ 1.60% to ≈ 1.55%.

$$
\text{Before:}\quad y = 2.00\% = 1.60\% + 0.40\% \quad (\mathrm{TP}=+40\ \text{bps})
$$

$$
\text{After:}\quad y = 1.50\% = 1.55\% - 0.05\% \quad (\mathrm{TP}=-5\ \text{bps})
$$

A $$\approx 45$$ bps compression in $$\mathrm{TP}$$ → demand-driven effect consistent with QE anticipation.

---

## Observable Market Signs

- **Yield curve:** bull flattening led by the long end.  
- **Swaps vs govies:** QE-induced shifts in spreads.  
- **Forward rates:** long forwards decline more than near-term forwards.  
- **Volatility:** lower term-structure risk premia → suppressed rate vol.

---

## One-Liner Summary

ECB QE expectations and easy funding created **duration scarcity**, which **compressed term premia**.  
Thus, yields fell even without lower expected short rates, signaling **exceptionally strong demand** for long-term debt.


## Introduction

A key challenge in **pension fund risk management** is that the **convexity of liabilities** is much larger than the **convexity of assets**.  
This structural mismatch drives **pro-cyclical hedging flows** and explains why funds are often said to be *short convexity*.

---

## Liability Convexity

- Pension liabilities are the **present value of long-dated cashflows** (benefit payments stretching 20–60 years).  
- Longer maturities imply:
  - **High duration** (often 15–25 years).  
  - **High convexity**, since convexity grows roughly with the square of maturity.  
- Liabilities are **option-free** (participants cannot prepay), so convexity is always **positive and large**.  

Mathematically, for option-free liabilities:

$$
C_L(y) = \frac{\sum t_i^2 \, CF_i \, e^{-y t_i}}{\sum CF_i \, e^{-y t_i}}
$$

This grows quickly as large $$t_i$$ dominate.

---

## Asset Convexity

Pension fund assets are typically more mixed and have **lower convexity**:

- **Bonds**: government/credit portfolios with maturities 5–12 years → convexity is smaller.  
- **Swaps**: receive-fixed swaps extend duration but have lower convexity than true 30y bonds.  
- **Credit/MBS**: callable or prepayable bonds can introduce **negative convexity**.  
- **Equities/Real assets**: no fixed cashflows, often treated as zero convexity in interest rate terms.  

Thus, even if duration is hedged, the convexity mismatch remains.

---

## Empirical Ordering

In practice:

$$
\text{Convexity}_{\text{liabilities}} \;>\; \text{Convexity}_{\text{assets}}
$$

- Liabilities: long-dated, option-free, strongly convex.  
- Assets: shorter maturity, possible negative convexity, and non-bond holdings.  

---

## Consequences for Risk Management

Because **liability convexity dominates**:

- When yields **fall**, liability DV01 rises faster than asset DV01.  
- This **widens the duration gap**.  
- To close the gap, funds must **buy more long bonds/swaps into rallies**.  
- Conversely, when yields **rise**, they must **sell**.  

This **buy-high, sell-low dynamic** is exactly the behavior of a **short-convexity position**.

---

## One-Liner Summary

Empirically, **pension fund liabilities always have higher convexity than assets**.  
This structural convexity gap explains why LDI strategies create **pro-cyclical hedging flows**, amplifying moves in long-term yields.
