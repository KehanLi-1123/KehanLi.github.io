## 1. The Apparent Paradox

It is often stated in practice that:

> *A 30-year zero-coupon bond has nearly zero delta risk to the 6-month tenor.*

At first glance, this appears contradictory, because mathematically the 30Y discount factor depends on **instantaneous forward rates (IFR)** over the entire interval from today to 30Y:

$$
DF(0,T) = \exp\left(-\int_0^T f(0,u)\,du\right), \quad T=30\text{Y}
$$

Since the integral includes the interval $[0, 6\text{M}]$, one might expect the 30Y bond to be sensitive to movements in the 6M rate.

This note explains why **dependence does not imply risk**.

---

## 2. Dependence vs. Risk

- **Mathematical dependence**:  
  The 30Y discount factor depends on all IFRs from today to 30Y.

- **Risk (delta / DV01)**:  
  Measures sensitivity of PV to *market-defined curve shocks*, not to arbitrary perturbations of the IFR function.

The distinction is crucial.

---

## 3. What “6M Delta” Means in Practice

On a trading desk, “6M delta” typically means:

$$
\Delta_{6M} = \frac{\partial PV}{\partial q_{6M}}
$$

where $q_{6M}$ is the **6M market quote** (deposit, OIS, FRA), **after re-calibrating the curve**, while keeping all other market quotes unchanged.

This is a **key-rate DV01**, not a raw functional derivative of $DF(0,30Y)$ with respect to $f(u)$.

---

## 4. Shock Conventions Matter

### 4.1 Key-Rate Bump with Re-Bootstrapping (Market Standard)

Procedure:
1. Shock the 6M instrument quote by 1 bp.
2. Rebuild the curve.
3. All other instruments (2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y swaps/bonds) are **held fixed**.

Implications:
- The 30Y par swap / bond constraint is still enforced.
- The solver adjusts only the **front-end segment**.
- Long-end zero rates and forwards remain essentially unchanged.

Result:
$$
\frac{\partial DF(0,30Y)}{\partial q_{6M}} \approx 0
$$

Hence:
> **30Y ZC has near-zero 6M bucket DV01.**

---

### 4.2 Node or IFR Bump without Re-Calibration (Theoretical Case)

If instead one applies a *pure IFR shock*:

$$
f(u) \rightarrow f(u) + \varepsilon, \quad u \in [0, 6\text{M}]
$$

with **no re-fitting to market instruments**, then:

$$
\Delta \ln DF(0,30Y)
\approx
-\int_0^{0.5} \varepsilon \, du
= -0.5 \varepsilon
$$

This produces a **non-zero but very small** sensitivity:

$$
\text{Impact ratio} \approx \frac{0.5}{30} \approx 1.7\%
$$

This is **not** how risk is measured or hedged in practice.

---

## 5. Why Re-Calibration Neutralizes the Long End

Your intuition is correct:

> Even though the 6M point is shocked, the curve recalibrates so that the 30Y constraint (market quote) remains unchanged.

More precisely:
- The **long-end instruments pin the long-end discount factors**.
- The solver does **not allow the long-end IFRs to move freely**.
- Adjustments are localized to the short-end region.

This is not “the long end moving up”, but rather:
> **the long end being constrained from moving at all.**

---

## 6. Time-Weighting Intuition

Even ignoring curve calibration, the short end has very small weight:

$$
\int_0^{30} f(u)\,du
=
\int_0^{0.5} f(u)\,du
+
\int_{0.5}^{30} f(u)\,du
$$

Only $\frac{0.5}{30}$ of the total discount exponent comes from the first 6 months.

Thus, short-end shocks are structurally weak drivers of long-end PV.

---

## 7. Key-Rate DV01 Concentration

| Instrument | 6M KRD | 5Y KRD | 30Y KRD |
|---------|------|------|-------|
| 6M ZC | Large | 0 | 0 |
| 5Y ZC | Small | Large | Small |
| 30Y ZC | ~0 | Small | Large |

This aligns with trading intuition:
> **Risk concentrates near maturity.**

---

## 8. Bottom Line

- ✔ The 30Y discount factor depends on IFRs from 0 to 30Y.
- ✔ Short-end IFRs contribute very little weight.
- ✔ Market risk is defined via **key-rate bumps with re-calibration**.
- ✔ Long-end instruments pin long-end discount factors.
- ✔ Therefore, a 30Y ZC has **near-zero delta to the 6M tenor**.

> **Dependence ≠ risk.**
