## Executive Summary

If you hold a **US Treasury 20Y+ Accumulating (Acc) ETF**, **there is no price drop on the coupon dates of the underlying Treasury bonds**.

This is because **coupons are reinvested inside the fund**, not distributed to investors.  
The ETF Net Asset Value (NAV) evolves smoothly, with coupon cash flows treated as an internal transfer between bond value and cash.

---

## What Does NAV Stand For?

**NAV** stands for **Net Asset Value**.

For an ETF or mutual fund, NAV represents the **per-share fair value** of the fund’s underlying assets after accounting for liabilities.

Formally:

$$
\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Total Assets} - \text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Number of Shares Outstanding}}
$$

---

## NAV in the Context of a US Treasury Bond ETF

For a US Treasury bond ETF, **Total Assets** typically include:

- Market value of Treasury bonds  
- Accrued interest  
- Cash holdings (e.g. coupon receipts, repo balances)

**Liabilities** include:

- Management fees payable  
- Operational and administrative expenses  

At the fund level, this can be written as:

$$
\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Bond Market Value} + \text{Cash} - \text{Liabilities}}{N}
$$

where $$N$$ is the number of ETF shares outstanding.

---

## NAV Behaviour on Coupon Dates (Acc ETF)

On a coupon payment date of the underlying Treasury bonds:

- Bond accrued interest resets, slightly reducing bond market value  
- Cash increases by the coupon amount received  

At the portfolio level:

$$
\Delta (\text{Bond Value}) + \Delta (\text{Cash}) \approx 0
$$

Therefore:

$$
\Delta \text{NAV} \approx 0 \quad \text{(ignoring yield curve movements)}
$$

This explains why an **Accumulating ETF does not experience a price drop on coupon dates**.

---

## NAV vs ETF Market Price

It is important to distinguish between:

| Term | Meaning |
|----|----|
| **NAV** | Economic value of the ETF’s assets per share |
| **ETF Market Price** | Trading price on the exchange |

For highly liquid US Treasury ETFs:

$$
\text{ETF Market Price} \approx \text{NAV}
$$

Any deviation is typically small and temporary due to the **creation–redemption arbitrage mechanism**.

---

## Key Interpretation for Investors

- NAV is the **anchor value** of an ETF  
- Accumulating ETFs embed coupon income into NAV growth  
- Coupon payments do **not** create mechanical price drops  
- Long-duration ETF risk remains driven by **duration and yield movements**, not coupon timing

## Background: Coupon Mechanics in Bonds vs ETFs


::contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}


Long-duration US Treasury ETFs (20Y+) hold portfolios of coupon-paying bonds, typically with maturities between 20 and 30 years. These bonds pay **semi-annual coupons**.

The key question is whether these coupon payments cause a **mechanical drop in ETF price**.

---

## Individual Coupon Bond Mechanics

For an individual coupon bond:

$$
P_{\text{dirty}} = P_{\text{clean}} + AI
$$

where:
- $$P_{\text{clean}}$$ = quoted bond price  
- $$AI$$ = accrued interest  

On the coupon payment date:
- Accrued interest resets to zero
- The **clean price drops** by approximately the coupon amount
- The **dirty price remains continuous**

This price drop is **mechanical**, not an economic loss.

---

## Accumulating ETF NAV Mechanics

For an **Accumulating (Acc) ETF**, the NAV can be written as:

$$
\text{NAV} = \text{Market Value of Bonds} + \text{Cash}
$$

### What happens on a coupon date?

1. **Underlying Treasuries pay coupons**
2. **Bond accrued interest resets**, slightly reducing bond prices
3. **Cash increases** by the same coupon amount
4. Cash is **reinvested** into Treasuries (or temporarily held)

The net effect on NAV is:

$$
\Delta \text{NAV} \approx 0 \quad \text{(ignoring yield movements)}
$$

This is an **internal accounting transfer**, not a payout.

---

## Why There Is No ETF Price Drop

The key intuition:

- Bond value ↓ due to accrual reset  
- Cash ↑ due to coupon receipt  

These two effects **offset exactly** at the fund level.

Unlike a single bond holder, ETF investors never see the coupon separately — it is automatically reinvested.

---

## Comparison: Accumulating vs Distributing ETFs

| Instrument | Coupon Date Effect |
|----------|--------------------|
| Individual bond | Clean price drops |
| Accumulating ETF | No price drop |
| Distributing ETF | ETF price drops by distribution |

For a **Distributing (Dist) ETF**, the relationship is:

$$
\text{ETF Price}_{\text{ex-date}} \approx \text{ETF Price}_{\text{cum-date}} - \text{Dividend}
$$

This does **not** apply to Acc ETFs.

---

## What *Can* Still Move the ETF Price on Coupon Dates?

Even on coupon dates, a 20Y+ Treasury ETF price can move due to:

- Yield curve shifts
- Long-end term premium changes
- Duration exposure
- Roll-down effects
- Index rebalancing
- Reinvestment timing differences

These effects dominate coupon mechanics, especially for long duration assets.

---

## Key Takeaway

> **A US Treasury 20Y+ Accumulating ETF does not experience a price drop on coupon dates.  
> Coupons are reinvested internally and reflected smoothly in the NAV.**

From a risk perspective, your exposure remains driven by **duration, DV01, and yield curve movements**, not by coupon payment dates.

---

## Practical Investor Interpretation

- Acc ETFs behave like **total return instruments**
- Coupon income is embedded in price appreciation
- There is no need to time purchases around coupon dates
- Long-end rate moves overwhelm coupon effects
